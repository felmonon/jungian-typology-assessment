The Jung Library

Where to start with Jung

Jung wrote some twenty volumes, and most of them are a bad place to begin. Pick the path that matches why you are here. Each step links to a short guide.

The short answer

Read Man and His Symbols first, then Memories, Dreams, Reflections. If you want the theory in his own words after that, read Two Essays on Analytical Psychology.

If you have never read Jung

You know the name and a few terms. You want to understand what he was saying without a background in psychology.

  1. Man and His SymbolsWritten to be understood. Read Jung’s own essay and von Franz’s part on individuation, and you have the outline.
  2. Memories, Dreams, ReflectionsThe life behind the outline. It shows what experiences the ideas were meant to explain.
  3. Modern Man in Search of a SoulShort essays in his own voice, on dreams, therapy and the stages of life.
  4. The Undiscovered SelfA hundred pages on why any of it matters outside the consulting room.

If you want the theory in order

You have had an introduction and now want Jung’s psychology in his own words, built up step by step.

  1. Two Essays on Analytical PsychologyThe whole structure in one volume. Start with the second essay.
  2. The Archetypes and the Collective UnconsciousThe two theoretical essays at the front, then the figures that interest you.
  3. Aion: Researches into the Phenomenology of the SelfChapters I to IV: the ego, shadow, anima and animus, and Self in their final form.
  4. Psychological TypesChapter X, for how the same psychology accounts for differences between people.

If you came from personality types

You know your four letters or your function stack and want to see what Jung meant before the systems were built.

  1. Psychological TypesChapter X is the source. Note how much of it is about the unconscious side of each type.
  2. Man and His SymbolsPlaces the functions inside the larger picture of dreams, shadow and individuation.
  3. Two Essays on Analytical PsychologyShows what typology was for: understanding the standpoint you look from.
  4. Aion: Researches into the Phenomenology of the SelfThe short chapter on the shadow, which is where the inferior function leads.

If you want Jung’s own experience

You are drawn to the visions, the mandalas and the tower, and want the first-hand record.

  1. Memories, Dreams, ReflectionsChapter VI tells the story of the crisis years in plain prose. Read it first.
  2. The Red Book: Liber NovusThe primary document. Read the editor’s introduction, then the text in short stretches.
  3. Psychology and AlchemyWhat he did next: the search for a historical parallel to what he had been through.

If you want the late work

You know the foundations and want the books of his last decade, on religion, synchronicity and the union of opposites.

  1. Aion: Researches into the Phenomenology of the SelfChapter V, on Christ as a symbol of the Self, opens the late themes.
  2. Answer to JobThe same argument, written with feeling. Read the preface carefully.
  3. Synchronicity: An Acausal Connecting PrincipleThe short lecture first, then the essay. His most speculative idea, in his own cautious terms.
  4. Mysterium ConiunctionisBegin with the last section, “The Conjunction”.

Before any book

If a book feels like too much today, the concept pages are built to be read in five minutes each. Six of them cover most of what people come looking for: shadow, archetypes, collective unconscious, individuation, anima, psychological types.

Questions people ask

What Carl Jung book should I read first?

For most people, Man and His Symbols. It is the one book Jung planned for general readers, it is illustrated, and it covers his main ideas. Memories, Dreams, Reflections is the best second book.

In what order should I read Jung’s Collected Works?

Start with volume 7, Two Essays on Analytical Psychology. Then read selected essays from volume 9i, the first four chapters of volume 9ii (Aion), and Chapter X of volume 6 (Psychological Types). Leave the alchemical volumes for last.

Should I start with The Red Book?

No. The Red Book is the private record of Jung’s inner experiment and assumes you already know why he undertook it. Read his memoir and one introductory work first.
