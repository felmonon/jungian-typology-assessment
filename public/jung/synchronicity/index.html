Synchronicity

Jung’s hypothesis that some coincidences are connected by meaning instead of cause.

Also searched as: meaningful coincidence, acausal connecting principle

In short

Synchronicity is Jung’s term for a meaningful coincidence: an inner event, such as a dream or thought, and an outer event that correspond in meaning without any causal link. He proposed it as a principle of connection alongside causality. It is a hypothesis, and it is not accepted by mainstream science.

What Jung meant

Jung defined synchronicity as the coincidence in time of two or more events that are not causally related and have the same or similar meaning. One event is psychic, the other physical. His best known example is a patient who was telling him a dream about a golden scarab when a scarab-like beetle tapped at the window. He caught it and handed it to her. The incident broke through her rigid rationalism in a way argument had not. He noted that the scarab is an ancient Egyptian symbol of rebirth, which fit the moment in her treatment. He also separated synchronicity from mere synchronism, meaning events that simply happen at the same time.

Where the idea came from

He had used the term in lectures from around 1930 and published the full essay in 1952, in a volume shared with the physicist Wolfgang Pauli, with whom he had corresponded for years. He drew on the Chinese I Ching, on J. B. Rhine’s card-guessing experiments, and on an astrological experiment of his own, which he admitted proved nothing statistically. He linked synchronicity to moments when an archetype is strongly activated, and speculated that psyche and matter might be two aspects of one underlying reality.

How to hold the idea

Synchronicity is the most speculative part of Jung’s work. It cannot be tested in the usual way, since by definition it does not repeat on demand, and critics attribute such experiences to chance and to the human habit of noticing hits and forgetting misses. Rhine’s results have not been reliably replicated. Jung himself called it a hypothesis and described the experiences as rare. It is reasonable to value the observation, that some coincidences strike people as deeply meaningful and affect them, without accepting his explanation.

Why it matters

For Jung the importance lay in meaning. A synchronistic event, in his account, tends to arrive at a turning point and to loosen a fixed attitude. That is a statement about its psychological effect, and it does not depend on settling what caused it.

Common misreadings

  • “Synchronicity means the universe is sending you signs.” Jung described a correspondence of meaning, not messages or guidance from an outside agent.
  • “It is the same as manifesting.” Manifesting claims that thoughts cause events. Synchronicity is defined by the absence of a causal link.
  • “Any coincidence is a synchronicity.” He reserved the word for rare coincidences tied to a charged inner state and felt as meaningful.
  • “Quantum physics has proven it.” Pauli took the idea seriously, but there is no accepted physical evidence for it.

Questions people ask

What is synchronicity in simple terms?

A meaningful coincidence between something in your mind and something in the outer world, with no causal connection between them. The concept comes from Carl Jung.

Is there scientific evidence for synchronicity?

No accepted evidence. It is a hypothesis that mainstream science does not use. Skeptics explain such experiences by chance and selective attention.

What is the scarab example?

A patient was describing a dream of a golden scarab when a similar beetle flew against Jung’s window. He used it as his main illustration of a meaningful coincidence.
