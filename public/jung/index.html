Structure of the psyche

The parts Jung distinguished, and how they relate.

The psyche Jung’s word for the whole of mental life, conscious and unconscious together. The ego The center of your conscious field: the “I” that knows, chooses and remembers. The persona The face you learn to show the world, useful until you mistake it for yourself. The shadow Everything about you that your conscious self-image leaves out, for better and for worse. The anima Jung’s name for the inner feminine image in a man, and the moods and fascinations it produces. The animus Jung’s name for the inner masculine image in a woman, heard as conviction and opinion. The Self The whole of who you are, conscious and unconscious, and the center that organizes it. The personal unconscious What your own life has forgotten, pushed aside or never quite noticed. The collective unconscious The inherited layer of the psyche that Jung believed all humans share. Archetypes Inherited patterns that shape how humans typically imagine, feel and act. Complexes Emotionally charged clusters in the unconscious that can take over how you react.

How the psyche moves

The processes that carry energy between conscious and unconscious.

Libido as psychic energy Jung’s wider meaning of libido: the general energy of mental life, not only sexual drive. Projection Seeing in other people what you cannot yet see in yourself. Compensation The psyche’s way of balancing a one-sided conscious attitude from below. Enantiodromia The tendency of any extreme to turn, in time, into its opposite. Synchronicity Jung’s hypothesis that some coincidences are connected by meaning instead of cause. Inflation When the ego swells by identifying with something much larger than itself. The tension of opposites Jung’s view that psychic life runs on polarity, and grows by holding both poles.

Growth and method

Individuation, and the methods Jung used to support it.

Individuation The lifelong process of becoming the particular, whole person you already potentially are. The transcendent function The process by which holding a conflict open produces a third position neither side expected. Active imagination Jung’s method of engaging the images of the unconscious while awake. Dreams For Jung, honest statements from the unconscious about how things actually stand.

Psychological types

Attitudes, functions, and the idea behind every type system since.

Psychological types Jung’s account of the habitual ways people orient themselves: two attitudes and four functions. Introversion and extraversion The direction your interest flows by habit: toward the outer object or the inner subject. The four functions Thinking, feeling, sensation and intuition: the four ways consciousness gets its bearings. The inferior function Your least developed function: slow, touchy, and the place where the unconscious gets in.

Archetypal figures

Recurring characters in myth, dream and fantasy.

The hero The pattern of leaving, struggling and winning something of value, read as the growth of consciousness. The trickster The fool and rule-breaker of myth, read by Jung as an image of the collective shadow. The mother archetype The inherited pattern behind every experience of mothering, nourishing and devouring alike. The wise old man The figure of insight and guidance who appears when the conscious mind has run out of answers. Puer aeternus The eternal youth: a divine child in myth, and a name for the adult who cannot land in life. The wounded healer The idea that a healer’s capacity to help is rooted in their own wound, and limited by it.

Symbols and sources

The images and traditions Jung read as maps of the psyche.

Symbols The best available expression of something that cannot yet be said any other way. The numinous The quality of awe, dread and fascination that marks contact with something greater than the ego. The mandala A circular image that Jung read as the psyche’s picture of its own wholeness and center. Put it to work

Three practices drawn from what Jung described. Each says what it is not and when to stop.

A shadow practice: working with a strong reactionA way of using moments of disproportionate irritation, envy or admiration to notice qualities you have not owned in yourself. It follows Jung’s account of projection.Practice A dream practice: recording and reading your dreamsA simple routine for writing dreams down and thinking about them in the order Jung worked: the dream as dreamed, the life context, your own associations, and then the question of what the dream adds to your conscious view. It is a way of paying attention.Practice Making room for your inferior functionA low-pressure way of giving your least developed function a small, regular place in your life. It follows Jung’s advice that the inferior function cannot be forced and has to be approached indirectly.Practice

How this library is made

Every quotation was checked against a published source and carries its citation. We separate what Jung wrote from what later writers added, say plainly where his ideas are contested or dated, and do not present his theories as established science.
