A shadow practice: working with a strong reaction

What it is

A way of using moments of disproportionate irritation, envy or admiration to notice qualities you have not owned in yourself. It follows Jung’s account of projection. It is a habit of honest attention. It does not remove the shadow, and it is not a substitute for therapy.

Before you start. Stop if this brings up overwhelming shame, memories of trauma, or thoughts of harming yourself, and talk to a qualified mental health professional. Do not use it to explain away mistreatment: a reaction can tell you something about yourself and the other person can still be in the wrong. This guide is educational and is not treatment.

The steps

  1. Catch the charge

    Choose one recent reaction that was stronger than the situation deserved. Irritation is the usual one. Envy, contempt and sudden admiration work as well. Pick something small. The colleague who annoys you is better material than the worst thing that ever happened to you.

  2. Describe the other person exactly

    Write three adjectives for what bothers or fascinates you. Be precise and uncharitable. “Smug” is more useful than “difficult”. Do not edit for fairness yet.

  3. Turn it around

    Ask three questions of each adjective. Where do I do this, perhaps in another area of life? Where do I secretly wish I could? When was I shamed out of it? Usually one of the three lands with a small jolt. That jolt is what you are looking for.

  4. Look for the value in it

    Jung held that the shadow contains good qualities that were never lived. Under “selfish” there may be the ability to say no. Under “arrogant”, the capacity to take up space. Name the unlived quality in neutral words.

  5. Carry it, quietly

    Do one small, conscious thing with the quality in your own life. Do not act the trait out, and do not announce your discovery to the other person. Then watch what happens the next time you meet them. A reaction that has been partly owned usually loses some heat.

  6. Keep a record

    Note the date, the adjectives and what landed. After a few weeks the same themes repeat, which shows you the shape of your own shadow more reliably than any single episode.

Where it comes from in Jung

The practice is built on the few pages of Aion in which Jung describes the shadow. He says there that its contents can be recognized with honest effort when they are one’s own personal traits, and that the stubborn cases are the ones bound up with projection, because then the cause of the emotion appears to lie in the other person. He also observes that emotion is the giveaway: it marks the place where adaptation is weakest.

Nothing in the steps above is a technique he prescribed. He described an attitude: notice, withdraw, admit, and bear the discomfort. The steps are one plain way of practicing it.

What it is not

It is not a purge. Jung did not think the shadow could be cleared out, and he distrusted anyone who claimed to have done it. It is not a set of prompts that leads to a finished result. It is also not self-attack. The aim is to become a little wider and less brittle, which means meeting what you find with some patience.

It does not mean that every strong reaction is a projection. Sometimes a person is simply behaving badly. The two can be true together, since by his account a projection needs a hook.

What to expect

Expect resistance. He called the recognition of the shadow a moral problem because it costs something to admit. The first honest look is usually unpleasant and partial. Expect also that it does not stay done. The same material returns in new company, a little more familiar each time.

How typology fits

In Jung’s typology the shadow speaks most readily through the inferior function. The people who get under your skin often lead with the function you have developed least: the intuitive is maddened by the stickler for detail, the thinker by the person who decides by feeling. Knowing which function that is for you narrows the search.

Questions people ask

What is shadow work?

A popular name for the effort to become aware of traits and impulses you have disowned. The idea comes from Jung’s concept of the shadow, although he did not use the phrase or offer a set method.

Is shadow work dangerous?

Gentle self-observation of everyday reactions is low risk for most people. Deliberately stirring up painful or traumatic material alone is different, and is better done with a qualified professional.

How long does shadow work take?

Jung treated it as never finished. The practical change is gradual: reactions are caught sooner and hold less power.
