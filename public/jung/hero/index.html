Archetypal figures

The hero

The pattern of leaving, struggling and winning something of value, read as the growth of consciousness.

Also searched as: the hero archetype, hero myth

In short

The hero is an archetypal figure found in myths everywhere: the one who leaves home, faces a monster and wins a treasure. Jung interpreted the pattern as an image of consciousness freeing itself from unconsciousness, and of the ego’s struggle to become independent. He also held that the heroic attitude must eventually be outgrown.

What Jung meant

Jung studied hero myths at length in the 1912 book later revised as Symbols of Transformation. He noticed a recurring outline: a miraculous or humble birth, early proofs of strength, a journey, a fight with a dragon or sea monster, often a period inside the monster’s belly, and a return with a prize that is hard to obtain. He read the whole as a drama of psychic energy. The monster is the pull back toward unconsciousness, which he associated with the devouring side of the mother archetype. The victory is the birth of an independent consciousness.

How it shows up

In individual life the pattern belongs above all to youth: leaving the parents, proving oneself, establishing an ego that can stand on its own. It appears in dreams of battles, quests and rescues, and in the heroes a person admires. Jung added that the dragon is real for everyone. In the passage quoted above, the difference between the hero and the victim is the outcome of the fight. Both have to face the same dragon.

The limit of the hero

Jung did not hold up the hero as a model for a whole life. A person who identifies with the figure is inflated (see inflation), and an ego that must always conquer cannot relate to the unconscious, which is the task of later life. He recorded a dream from December 1913 in which he took part in killing Siegfried, the Germanic hero, and understood it to mean that his own heroic attitude, the will to prevail, had to be sacrificed. Individuation asks the hero to give way to something larger than the ego.

Jung versus later use

The “hero’s journey” as a set of stages comes from Joseph Campbell’s The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949), which drew on Jung and was later simplified into a formula for screenwriters. Erich Neumann developed the theme in another direction, as a history of consciousness. Jung’s own interest was narrower and more psychological: what the myth shows about the cost of becoming conscious.

Common misreadings

  • “The hero’s journey is Jung’s model.” The staged journey is Joseph Campbell’s. Jung supplied the psychological reading that Campbell built on.
  • “The goal is to become the hero of your own life.” Jung saw identification with the hero as inflation, and thought the heroic stance had to be given up in the second half of life.
  • “The dragon is an outside enemy.” In his reading it is one’s own inertia and unconsciousness.

Where to read it in Jung

  • Symbols of Transformation (CW 5)Part Two, especially “The Origin of the Hero” and “The Dual Mother”.
  • The Archetypes and the Collective Unconscious (CW 9i)“The Psychology of the Child Archetype”, on the child-hero.
  • Man and His SymbolsPart 2, “Ancient Myths and Modern Man” by Joseph L. Henderson. The most accessible treatment.

Questions people ask

What is the hero archetype in Jungian psychology?

A universal pattern of struggle and victory that Jung read as the ego’s emergence from unconsciousness. It is most active in the first half of life, when independence has to be won.

Is the hero’s journey from Jung or Campbell?

The stage-by-stage hero’s journey is Joseph Campbell’s, published in 1949. Campbell was strongly influenced by Jung’s earlier work on hero myths.
