How the psyche moves

Compensation

The psyche’s way of balancing a one-sided conscious attitude from below.

Also searched as: self-regulation of the psyche

In short

Compensation is Jung’s principle that the unconscious balances the conscious attitude. Whatever consciousness overvalues, ignores or excludes gathers weight in the unconscious and pushes back, most visibly in dreams. He saw it as the self-regulation of the psyche, comparable to the way the body maintains equilibrium.

What Jung meant

Consciousness works by focusing, and focus excludes. Jung reasoned that what is excluded does not disappear. It accumulates in the unconscious and forms a counterweight to the conscious position. The more one-sided the conscious attitude, the stronger the counterposition becomes. He took the term from Alfred Adler, who had used it for the way people make up for a felt inferiority, and widened it into a general law of the psyche. He stressed that as a rule the unconscious does not run counter to consciousness. It balances and supplements, and turns hostile mainly when it is persistently ignored.

How it shows up

Dreams were his main evidence. A person who thinks too highly of themselves dreams of humiliation. A person who undervalues themselves may dream of unexpected stature. Someone living entirely in the head dreams of animals and floods. The dream does not simply oppose. It adds what is missing so that the picture is more complete. Compensation also appears in moods, slips, symptoms, and sudden attractions to people or ideas unlike one’s usual self.

When it is ignored

A compensation that is noticed can be taken into account, and the attitude corrects itself. One that is refused grows. Jung described a sequence in which the counterposition first disturbs conscious performance and finally breaks through as its opposite, a reversal he called enantiodromia. Neurosis, in his view, is often a compensation that has not been understood.

Why it matters

The principle changes how inner disturbance is read. Instead of asking only what caused a dream or symptom, Jung asked what it is for: which conscious attitude does it answer, and what is it trying to add? The question assumes that the psyche is purposive and that its disturbances carry information. It is a working assumption, and he treated it as one. It also grounds his typology, where the inferior function compensates the dominant one. The habit that follows is simple: ask of any strong dream, mood or slip what one-sidedness it might be answering.

Common misreadings

  • “Compensation means the dream shows the opposite of the truth.” It shows what the conscious view leaves out. Sometimes that confirms the conscious attitude instead of opposing it.
  • “It is the same as Adler’s overcompensation.” Adler meant striving to overcome inferiority. Jung meant an automatic balancing between conscious and unconscious.
  • “The unconscious always knows better.” Jung said it complements consciousness. It needs the conscious mind to evaluate what it offers.

Questions people ask

What is compensation in Jungian psychology?

The principle that the unconscious balances a one-sided conscious attitude, supplying what consciousness leaves out. Jung saw it as the psyche regulating itself.

How do dreams compensate?

By presenting the side of a situation the dreamer has overlooked or undervalued. Jung read a dream by asking what conscious attitude it corrects or completes.
